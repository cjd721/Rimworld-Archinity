Archinity · Progression map v2 · Neolithic & Medieval

A hundred and one nodes,
each one designed

The research tree for the Neolithic and Medieval eras, built from nothing. Every node names what it hands the player, what dependency it trades, and why it exists at that point in the story. Nothing here is derived from how any mod currently bundles its research.

The mods are a parts bin, not a blueprint. Every model, texture, stat block and def in the load order is raw material to be renamed, restatted, re-parented, split, merged or thrown out. Where the design wants something the bin does not contain, it is named anyway and marked for authoring.

Pacing and balance are deliberately absent. No point costs, no day counts, no research amounts. Prerequisites are shown as ordering intent — the exact edge list is a balance-pass artefact, not a design one.

Companion to progression-map.html, which holds the narrative for the five leaps and stays as written. This document replaces only its research tree.

01 · The correction

The last pass designed a patch. This one designs a tree.

Version one's tree section is a good audit and a bad design. It took the shipped research projects as the units of work and asked what each one currently gives, where the collisions are, and what is missing. That question produces a to-do list against Medieval Overhaul. It cannot produce a progression, because the bundles were never authored for this playthrough and no amount of correcting them will make them fit it.

v1 · the false premise

Growing hands over twenty-four plants

Because VFET_Agriculture patches sowResearchPrerequisites across Core and Vanilla Cooking Expanded, and every one of those plants resolves at once. The node was described by reading what the patch does.

v2 · the design

Growing hands over four plants: a grain, a root, a fibre, a fodder. One per role. The player can hold the whole crop list in their head on day three, and every later agriculture node adds a role — a crop that is an input, a crop that is a material — never a synonym for one they already grow. The other twenty are not cut for arithmetic; they are cut because a second root vegetable is not a discovery.

v1 · the false premise

Butchery has nothing left to unlock

Because the butcher table already arrived with VFET_Construction and the butcher spot with VFET_Hunting. The finding was real. The framing — this node is now empty, should it be deleted? — was not a design question.

v2 · the design

Construction unlocks walls, doors and roofs, and nothing else. Not the stove, not the butcher table, not the palisade, not the cavalry spikes. Every one of those belongs to the node whose beat it is. The collision does not need resolving, because in a tree that was designed rather than inherited it never occurs.

v1 · the false premise

Sixteen projects unlock zero ThingDefs

Presented as a defect found in the database. It is a defect — but it is a defect in somebody else's tree, and correcting it is bookkeeping on a structure this project has no reason to keep.

v2 · the design

A node does not have to unlock a building to be worth researching. Several of the best nodes below unlock a verb, a recipe class, or a relationship with the world and nothing placeable at all. What a node must never be is a toll — something every player pays and no player wants for itself.

02 · The design law

Eight rules. Five carried from v1, three added by this pass.

A node that violates one is wrong regardless of how good it sounds. The three new rules are the ones that were doing the work implicitly and needed saying out loud.

L1

One core bench per domain, upgraded forever. Augments persist.

The hearth cooked on in the Neolithic is the same hearth, upgraded, on the gravship. Every augment bolted to it carries across every upgrade. The player never manages five buildings for one domain and never hunts five bill tabs for one recipe.

Big unlocks are bench upgrades. Small unlocks are augments. That is the whole shape of production progression, and §03 lays out every ladder in one table.

L2

The surface grows. The complexity stays linear.

Each food tier adds exactly one generic ingredient class — never a named crop. “Any two vegetables,” never “corn and potatoes.” Wood is wood, cloth is cloth, metal is metal. No plank step, no board step, no ingot step, no spinning step.

Two exceptions are granted and no more: flour and cheese. Both earn it by being genuinely different from their input, and both are made by a place the player builds once rather than a bill they operate. Anything still demanding busywork in the Spacer era does not ship.

L3

Every rung is a real improvement.

Hide → cured → boiled → hardened → scale → mail → plate. Seven rungs, each better on the stats that decide a fight. A rung that costs more, needs more research and is not better should not exist. A tier whose only function is to be skipped is a bug.

L4

A resource must evolve with the player or it does not exist.

Does this thing's use case still matter three eras from now, or does it become permanent noise in trade stock and quest rewards? A resource that stops mattering is worse than one that never existed, because it keeps costing attention forever. Salt fails. Linen fails. A medieval-only supermetal fails.

L5

Two classes of research, gated differently.

Spine — you must have it to play. Ordinary resource and item requirements, never a fetch quest.

Quality-of-life — the trough, the scarecrow, the catapult, the mine shaft, the crane. Rare, high-value, and it gates nothing. The resource cost can be near zero; the going and getting is the cost. Two or three per era.

L6

A tier is a research plus an augment, never a new material.new

This is the rule that keeps the seven-rung armour ladder from becoming the wood chain. Hardened leather is a recipe you unlock, not a resource you convert. The cuirass costs plain leather and requires the boilpot to be standing; there is no rawhide bill, no hardleather stack, no intermediate item in the stockpile.

The same rule kills the iron ingot. Ore smelts to steel and steel is what a sword costs. It also permits materials that arrive as a straight substitution — silk is cloth that is better, usable anywhere cloth is usable, with nothing to relearn.

L7

Every node answers: what does the colony stop depending on?new

RimWorld tech is lateral. Hydroponics does not make you stronger — it swaps a dependency on soil and weather for a dependency on power. That is why an open tech tree does not break the game: nothing in it is a reward, it is all reconfiguration.

If the answer to “what does this trade” is “nothing, it is just better,” the node is a ladder rung and does not ship. Every card below carries its trade on a separate line so the rule is checkable at a glance rather than assumed.

L8

Some nodes exist to create demand, not capability.new

Capability is abundant and demand is scarce. The mid-game sags because the world stops asking new kinds of question, not because the player runs out of things to build.

So a handful of nodes here produce no capability at all — they produce other people's problems. Carrier birds let factions ask you for things. Siege engines make the world map somewhere you can go. Standing & Embassy makes helping one neighbour into refusing another. Heraldry converts a colony into a name that other colonies have opinions about. These are the highest-value nodes in the project and they should be protected in any cut.

Two rows have a deliberate hole in M1 and they are the point of the leap. Ranged and Siege get nothing when metal arrives, because M1's whole job is to remake what the colony already has in a new material. 05 · The tree

A hundred and one nodes, in order.

Each card: what it hands over, the dependency it trades, and why it sits where it sits. Prerequisites are ordering intent. Some “why” lines are arguments and some are simply this follows from that — both are legitimate reasons for a node to exist, and pretending otherwise produces a tree where every node is straining to be a moment.

Spine — you must have it to play. QoL hunt — gates nothing. The going and getting is the cost. Optional — skip it, lose no capability. Moment — one of the four named beats. Want — the design asks for it; nothing in the load order supplies it.

  • buildpyre · gibbet cage · skullspike

Trades — build cost and ritual time for ideology precepts the colony has already committed to.

Optional, gates nothing. It exists because the campaign's own altar arrives from a quest rather than from research, and the tribe that finds it needs somewhere to put the religion it is about to build around a machine it does not understand.

07 · Calls made in this pass

Six decisions taken on taste. Each is reversible; none is arbitrary.

Recorded here rather than buried in a node, because these are the places where a different answer would produce a materially different tree.

C1

Four crops in Growing, not twenty-four.

The full loaded plant pool across Core, Medieval Overhaul and Vanilla Cooking Expanded is roughly ninety. This design uses about eighteen across four agriculture nodes.

the reasoning

A plant earns its slot by filling a role no other plant fills — a grain, a fibre, an input, a material — never by being a second vegetable. The test is whether the player would ever choose between two of them for a reason. If not, the second one is a longer sowing menu and nothing else.

C2

Flour and cheese are the only processing chains.

Everything else — wood, cloth, leather, metal, silk — goes from raw to used in one step, and the tier is carried by research plus an augment rather than by an intermediate item.

the reasoning

Both survive the Spacer test: a mill and a cheese press are still coherent objects on a gravship, and a plank bench is not. Both are also places rather than bills — built once, ignored afterwards. Any future candidate has to clear both bars, and almost nothing does.

C3

M1 gets no new bow and no new siege engine.

The widest leap in the project adds nineteen nodes and leaves two whole domains untouched.

the reasoning

M1's job is everything you own, again, in metal. Adding new domains alongside that dilutes the one idea the leap has. It also leaves M2 with a headline — the crossbow — that would otherwise have to compete with mail. A leap with a spare rung is healthier than a leap with a crowded one.

C4

The bellows is spine. The grinding wheel is the hunt.

The Ideology document floats gating the bellows behind a quest and then talks itself out of it in the same paragraph.

the reasoning

The bellows gates half the armoury, so gating it behind a hunt makes the hunt a tax. The grinding wheel improves everything and gates nothing, which is exactly the shape L5 asks for — and it keeps paying until the end of the campaign. Same augment slot, same forge, opposite gating class.

C5

Firearms are worse than crossbows.

Gunpowder is the M3 beat and the weapons it unlocks are slower, less accurate and more dangerous to their owner than the M2 ranged line.

the reasoning

They punch through plate, and that is the entire point. The player's own research invalidates the armour investment they just completed — a qualitative shift that forces a rethink rather than a rebuild, arriving from inside the tech tree instead of from a difficulty curve. It is the cleanest piece of regime change the two eras can produce.

C6

Rome is optional; the Roman engineering is not.

Bath house, stage and ceremonial dress are optional nodes. Masonry, cement, earthworks, the shield wall and the catapult are spine.

the reasoning

A stone-and-leather civilisation with discipline and siege is the late Neolithic — that is the N2 fantasy and it cannot be optional. What can be optional is the visible Roman-ness: the marble baths and the togas. A player who wants the austere read skips three nodes and loses a mood buffer, not a capability.
